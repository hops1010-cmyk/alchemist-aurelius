# The Alchemist, Aurelius

## Book One: The Casting of Stone

### Chapter 1: The Morning the King Came

The morning the king came, Aurelius was building a wall. Not a wall of stone, exactly, though stone was involved. It was a wall of understanding — a wall made of ratios, of load-bearing calculations, of the quiet knowledge that a structure must know its own weight before it can hold anyone else's. He had been at it since before dawn, chalking lines on the floor of his workshop, a converted barn on the edge of a village whose name had been forgotten by everyone except the people who lived there and the postman who visited on Tuesdays.

The workshop smelled of lime and iron and the particular sweetness of fresh-cut oak. Aurelius liked oak. He liked the way it held a nail and the way it complained when you bent it too far, a sound like a throat clearing. He had been an alchemist once, in the sense that the word had meant something different then — not gold from lead, but strength from weakness, structure from chaos. He had turned mud into mortar, sand into glass, doubt into certainty. The villagers called him the stone man. The children called him the castle man. The king, as it turned out, called him something else entirely.

The king arrived without announcement, which was the king's way. He came with six guards and a carriage and a look on his face that suggested he had been thinking about something for a long time and had finally decided to act on it. Aurelius set down his chalk. He did not bow, because he had never bowed, and the king did not seem to mind, because the king had not come for ceremony.

"You are the one who builds strong castles," the king said. It was not a question.

"I am the one who builds castles that stand," Aurelius said. "Strong is a word other people use."

The king looked at the chalk lines on the floor. He looked at the models on the shelf — miniature towers, bridges, a keep with a double wall. He looked at the mortar samples, labeled in Aurelius's careful hand. He looked at the oven, the bellows, the pile of limestone waiting to be burned into quicklime. He looked at all of it, and then he looked at Aurelius, and his face did something complicated.

"I need castles," the king said. "Strong ones. Ones that stand when the siege comes. Ones that do not crumble because a man forgot to account for the weight of his own ambition."

Aurelius wiped his hands on his apron. "Then you need an engineer, not an alchemist."

"I need you," the king said, and the six guards shifted, and the carriage horse stamped, and the morning suddenly felt smaller than it had a moment before.

### Chapter 2: The Workshop

The workshop was Aurelius's world. It had been his father's before him, and his father's before that, back to a time when the village was a settlement and the settlement was a camp and the camp was a fire in the dark. The barn had been rebuilt three times — once after a storm, once after a fire, once after Aurelius himself had knocked out the back wall to make room for a larger oven. Each rebuild had taught him something. The storm taught him about drainage. The fire taught him about ventilation. The knock-out taught him about load paths, about how a wall that looks solid can be hollow at its heart, and how a hollow wall will hold until it doesn't, and when it doesn't, it takes everything with it.

He had seven children and a wife, and they lived in the house attached to the barn, and the house was warm, and the kitchen smelled of bread on baking days and of soup on other days, and the children were loud in the way that children are loud — not because they are trying to be, but because the world is new and they are announcing it. James, eighteen, was already taller than Aurelius and already quieter, a boy who watched and listened and said little, which Aurelius recognized as the beginning of wisdom. Henry, fourteen, was the opposite — all noise and motion, hands everywhere, questions about everything. William, ten, was the reader, the one who sat in corners with books and emerged with facts. Charles, six, was the dreamer, the one who built castles from blocks and then knocked them down and built them again. Archibald, two, was the baby, the one who sat on laps and grabbed at beards and laughed at everything.

Elizabeth and Anne were twenty-one and eighteen, twins in the way that twins are — not identical, but paired, two halves of a conversation that started before they were born. Elizabeth was the elder by eleven minutes, and she never let Anne forget it. Anne was the younger, and she never let Elizabeth forget that she was the one who had been born second, which she considered an advantage because it meant she had had more time to prepare for the world.

And then there was Furball. Furball was a kitten, small and grey and perpetually confused, who had appeared in the barn one morning and never left. She slept in the mortar trough. She chased chalk lines. She sat on Aurelius's shoulder while he worked and purred into his ear, a sound like a small engine idling. The children loved her. The wife tolerated her. Aurelius, who had never been a cat person, found himself unable to imagine the workshop without her.

This was the world the king walked into. This was the world the king decided to take.

### Chapter 3: The Conversation

The king sat on a stool. The stool creaked. The king did not notice, or chose not to.

"Tell me about castles," he said.

Aurelius told him. He told him about walls and their thickness, about the difference between a curtain wall and a keep, about how a round tower resists siege better than a square one because a ram cannot find a corner to bite. He told him about foundations, about how a castle is only as strong as the ground it stands on, and how the ground is only as strong as the water table beneath it, and how the water table is only as strong as the rain that feeds it. He told him about angles — the angle of repose, the angle of a batter, the angle at which an arrow looses and lands. He told him about lime, about how it must be burned and slaked and mixed and cured, and how rushing any of those steps will produce a wall that looks solid and is not.

The king listened. He listened the way a man listens when he has already decided and is only confirming. Aurelius recognized this, because he had listened the same way to clients who wanted a wall built fast and cheap and were surprised when it fell.

"You will come with me," the king said. "You will build for me. You will build castles that stand, and you will not stop until I tell you to stop."

"And my family?"

The king paused. The pause was long enough for Aurelius to hear the chickens outside, the wind in the eaves, Furball's purr from the mortar trough.

"Your family will be provided for," the king said. "They will want for nothing."

"That is not what I asked."

The king stood. The stool creaked again. "You will come with me, Aurelius. This is not a request."

It was not a request. Aurelius knew this the way he knew the weight of a stone — not because someone told him, but because the physics of the situation were undeniable. The king had six guards. The king had a carriage. The king had the authority to take what he wanted, and what he wanted was the man who knew how to build strong castles.

Aurelius looked at the chalk lines on the floor. He looked at the models on the shelf. He looked at the mortar trough, where Furball slept, and the kitchen door, where his wife was probably starting lunch, and the window, where he could see James crossing the yard with a load of firewood.

"I will come," he said. "But I will not be happy about it."

The king smiled. It was not a kind smile. It was the smile of a man who has gotten what he wanted and does not need to pretend otherwise.

### Chapter 4: The Departure

They left at noon. The king did not give Aurelius time to say goodbye properly, which Aurelius later understood was deliberate — a goodbye gives a person a chance to change their mind, and the king did not want Aurelius to change his mind. Aurelius was allowed to pack a bag. He packed his tools, his chalk, his measuring lines, his notes. He did not pack clothes, because the king said clothes would be provided. He did not pack food, because the king said food would be provided. He did not pack Furball, because Furball was a cat and cats do not pack.

He knelt by the mortar trough. Furball opened one eye, assessed the situation, and closed it again. She did not move. She did not know that the man who had been there every morning for two years was about to leave. She knew only that the trough was warm and the sun was coming through the window and the world was, for the moment, acceptable.

Aurelius's wife stood in the doorway. She did not cry. She had cried enough in her life to know that crying in front of the king's guards was a form of surrender, and she was not surrendering. She looked at Aurelius with an expression that contained everything — love, fear, anger, pride, the particular grief of a woman who knows her husband is leaving and cannot stop him and will not ask him to stay because asking would be worse than the leaving.

"Build something worth the cost," she said.

"I will," he said.

James carried his bag to the carriage. Henry stood in the yard and did not speak. William had a book open but was not reading it. Charles was building a castle from blocks on the step, and when Aurelius walked past, Charles looked up and said, "Will you build me a real one?"

"Yes," Aurelius said. "I will build you a real one."

Archibald, two years old, did not understand any of it. He sat on the step and laughed at a chicken. Elizabeth and Anne stood together, as they always did, and Elizabeth said, "We will hold the workshop." And Anne said, "We will hold everything."

Aurelius got into the carriage. The king's guards closed the door. The carriage lurched forward. The village shrank behind him — the barn, the house, the yard, the chickens, the step where Charles was still building, the step where Archibald was still laughing. Furball, in the mortar trough, opened one eye again, and then closed it, and slept.

### Chapter 5: The Road

The road was long and the carriage was uncomfortable and the king did not speak for the first three hours, which Aurelius appreciated, because he was not in the mood for conversation. He was in the mood for grief, and grief requires silence, and the king, for all his faults, seemed to understand this.

They stopped at an inn. The inn was adequate. The food was adequate. The king ate in a private room. Aurelius ate in the common room, alone, surrounded by travelers who did not know who he was and did not care. He ordered bread and cheese and a small beer, and he ate slowly, and he thought about his family, and he thought about Furball, and he thought about the chalk lines on the floor of his workshop, and he wondered if anyone would finish the wall he had started.

The answer, he knew, was no. No one would finish it. The wall would sit there, half-drawn, and eventually someone would sweep the chalk away, and the lines would be gone, and the wall would exist only in his memory. This is what happens to unfinished work. It does not wait. It fades.

He slept badly. The bed was too soft. He was used to a hard mattress, to the feel of straw and wool, to the specific resistance of a bed that pushes back. This bed swallowed him. He dreamed of chalk lines that erased themselves, of mortar that would not set, of walls that leaned and leaned and never fell but never stood either. He dreamed of Furball, purring in a trough that was not there.

In the morning, they were back on the road. The king spoke this time.

"I have a site," he said. "A hill. It overlooks a valley and a river and the approach from the north. The enemy will come from the north. They always come from the north."

"Then you need a castle that faces north," Aurelius said.

"I need a castle that faces everything."

Aurelius looked out the window. The landscape was changing. The village was gone. The fields were different. The trees were different. He was in a different country now, or at least a different part of the same one, and the difference was in the soil, in the light, in the way the wind moved. He was a stranger here, and strangers build walls differently. They build them thicker, because they do not trust the ground.

### Chapter 6: The Site

The site was a hill. The king was right about that. It rose from the valley like a fist, and from its top you could see the river to the east and the forest to the west and the road to the north, the road the enemy would come down. The wind was strong here. The soil was thin. The bedrock was close to the surface, which was good, because bedrock does not shift, and a castle built on bedrock will stand long after the builder is dead.

Aurelius walked the hill. He walked it the way he walked every site — slowly, carefully, with his eyes on the ground and his hands on the stone. He tested the soil with a stick. He tasted the water. He watched the wind. He noted the angle of the sun at midday and the direction of the prevailing wind and the way the river curved at the base of the hill. He did all of this in silence, and the king's guards watched him in silence, and the king watched him with an expression that was, for the first time, not entirely confident.

"The ground is good," Aurelius said. "The bedrock is close. The water table is low. The wind will be a problem on the north face, but it can be managed."

"Can you build here?"

"I can build anywhere. The question is whether you can afford what I build."

The king did not like this answer. Aurelius could see that. But the king also could not argue with it, because it was true, and truth is the one thing a king cannot command away.

"How long?" the king asked.

"A castle is not a house," Aurelius said. "A house takes months. A castle takes years. A castle that will stand against a siege takes longer, because every wall must be tested, every angle must be calculated, every stone must be chosen. If you want it fast, I can build you a wall. If you want it strong, I must build you a castle. These are different things."

"How long?" the king asked again.

"Three years. Maybe four. Maybe five, if the winter is hard."

The king looked at the hill. He looked at the valley. He looked at the road to the north, where the enemy would come. He looked at Aurelius.

"Three years," he said. "Not five."

"Then pray for mild winters," Aurelius said.

### Chapter 7: The Plan

Aurelius spent the first month planning. He drew. He measured. He calculated. He filled notebooks with numbers and diagrams and notes in his careful hand. He walked the hill every morning, and every morning he found something new — a crack in the bedrock, a pocket of clay, a spot where the wind hit differently. He incorporated each finding into the plan, and the plan grew, and the plan changed, and the plan became something that no one else could have produced, because no one else had his eyes, his hands, his knowledge of how stone and earth and wind interact.

The plan called for a keep at the top of the hill, round, with walls twelve feet thick. It called for a curtain wall around the keep, lower, with towers at intervals. It called for a gatehouse on the north face, the most vulnerable side, with a portcullis and a murder hole and a killing ground. It called for a well inside the curtain wall, because a castle without water is a tomb. It called for a granary, because a castle without food is a grave. It called for a chapel, because a castle without faith is a fortress, and a fortress is not a home.

The king approved the plan. He approved it the way kings approve things — by saying "yes" and walking away, leaving the details to someone else. Aurelius did not mind. He preferred to be left alone with his work. He preferred the chalk and the stone and the wind to the king's attention, which was heavy and warm, like a hand on the back of the neck.

### Chapter 8: The Workers

The workers came from the villages. They were farmers and laborers and craftsmen, men who knew how to swing a hammer and carry a stone and mix mortar. They did not know how to build a castle. That was Aurelius's job. Their job was to do what he told them, and to do it well, and to not ask too many questions.

Aurelius was not a tyrant. He was a teacher. He showed them how to lay a stone, how to check it for cracks, how to tap it with a hammer and listen for the sound that meant it was sound. He showed them how to mix mortar — not too wet, not too dry, not too much lime, not too little. He showed them how to read a wall, how to look at a course of stone and see whether it was level, whether it was plumb, whether it was tied into the course below. He showed them, and they learned, and the castle began to rise.

The work was hard. The work was slow. The work was the kind of work that does not look like progress until suddenly it does — a wall that was an idea becomes a wall that is a fact, and the fact is taller than the idea was, and the fact is heavier, and the fact is real in a way that the idea never was.

Aurelius worked alongside them. He did not sit in a chair and point. He carried stones. He mixed mortar. He laid courses. He was the first one there in the morning and the last one to leave at night, and the workers respected him for it, because workers always respect the man who does the work, and they never respect the man who only watches.

### Chapter 9: Letters

The letters came once a month. They were carried by a rider who came from the village, and they were written in his wife's careful hand, and they were the only connection Aurelius had to the life he had left behind. He read them in his tent, at night, by candlelight, and he read them slowly, because each word was a thread and the threads made a fabric and the fabric was his family.

Elizabeth wrote about the workshop. She had taken over the management of it, and she was good at it — better than Aurelius had expected, though he should not have been surprised, because Elizabeth had always been the one who organized, who planned, who kept things running. She wrote about the orders that came in, the repairs that needed doing, the clients who wanted walls and bridges and wells. She wrote about the accounts, which she kept in a ledger she had found in the back room, and about the money, which was not enough but was not nothing.

Anne wrote about the children. James had taken a job with the miller. Henry had broken his arm falling off a fence and was recovering. William had read three books in a week and could not stop talking about them. Charles had built a castle from blocks that was so tall it fell over and hit Archibald, who laughed. Furball had had kittens — four of them, grey like her, and the children had named them all.

Aurelius read the letters twice. Once for the news, once for the feeling. The feeling was harder. It was the feeling of being far away and unable to return, of being needed somewhere else and unable to be there. It was the feeling of a man who has chosen his work over his family, even though the choice was not entirely his.

He wrote back. He wrote about the castle, about the walls, about the workers and the stone and the wind. He wrote about the hill and the valley and the river. He did not write about the king, because the king was always there, watching, and Aurelius did not want to put his thoughts about the king into a letter that might be read by someone else.

He wrote to Furball, too, though he knew she could not read. He wrote about the mortar trough and the chalk lines and the sound of her purr. He wrote it for himself, because writing about Furball made the workshop feel closer, and closeness was what he needed.

### Chapter 10: The Winter

The first winter was hard. The wind came from the north, as the king had said it would, and it brought snow and ice and a cold that got into the stone and made it brittle. Aurelius had planned for this. The walls were thick enough. The mortar was mixed for cold weather — more lime, less water, a slower cure. The workers wore furs and drank hot tea and complained, and Aurelius complained with them, because complaining is a form of solidarity and solidarity is what keeps a crew together in the cold.

The king visited once a week. He came on horseback, with two guards, and he stood on the hill and looked at the castle and asked questions. "How high?" "How thick?" "When will it be done?" Aurelius answered the questions, and the king nodded, and the king left, and Aurelius went back to work. The king did not stay long. The wind was too cold for him, and the castle was too unfinished for his liking, and he had other things to attend to — other castles, other wars, other men to command.

Aurelius preferred it this way. The king's presence was a weight, like a stone on a wall, and the wall was stronger without it.

The winter passed. The spring came. The castle was taller than it had been, and the walls were thicker, and the keep was beginning to take shape. The workers were better at their jobs. The mortar was setting well. The stone was good. The plan was working.

Aurelius wrote to his wife: "The castle is rising. The walls are strong. I miss you. I miss the workshop. I miss Furball. I miss the chalk lines and the mortar trough and the sound of the children in the yard. I miss everything. But the castle is rising, and it will stand, and when it stands, I will come home."

He did not know if this was true. He did not know if the king would let him go home. He did not know if the castle would ever be finished, or if the enemy would come before it was done, or if the walls would hold when the siege came. He knew only that he was building, and that building was what he did, and that the building was good.

### Chapter 11: The Apothecary

The apothecary was the other half of Aurelius's life, and it was the half the king did not know about, or did not care about, which amounted to the same thing. It sat behind the workshop, a small stone room with a single window and a door that locked from the inside. The shelves were lined with jars — glass, ceramic, clay — each labeled in Aurelius's hand. Tinctures of yarrow and comfrey. Salves of beeswax and calendula. A tincture of willow bark that eased the ache in old joints. A poultice of plantain that drew the sting from a bee. A distillation of rosemary that cleared the head and sharpened the eye.

Aurelius was not only a builder of castles. He was a maker of remedies, a reader of humors, a man who understood that a wall and a body had more in common than most people thought. Both needed structure. Both needed balance. Both needed the right materials in the right proportions, and both would fail if you rushed the cure.

He had learned the apothecary from his mother, who had learned it from her mother, back to a time when the village had no doctor and the apothecary was the doctor, the pharmacist, and the midwife all in one. His mother had been a woman of quiet competence, the kind of woman who could look at a child's throat and know, before the child opened its mouth, whether it was a cold or something worse. She had taught Aurelius to look, to listen, to smell, to taste. She had taught him that the body speaks, and that the apothecary's job is to hear it.

In the apothecary, Aurelius felt like himself. The castle site was the king's world. The workshop was his father's world. The apothecary was his own. Here, he was not the stone man or the castle man or the alchemist. He was the man who made the tinctures, who mixed the salves, who knew which herb to pick and which to leave and which to dry and which to use fresh. Here, the work was small and precise and personal. A wall could be ten feet thick. A tincture could be ten drops. Both, if done right, could save a life.

Furball spent her time in the apothecary, not the workshop. The workshop was too loud, too dusty, too full of the sound of hammers and the smell of lime. The apothecary was quiet. The apothecary smelled of herbs and honey and the faint sweetness of dried lavender. Furball would sit on the shelf among the jars, her grey fur blending with the ceramic, and watch Aurelius work. She had a particular interest in the willow bark tincture, which she would sniff at length before turning away, as if she had considered it and found it wanting.

The children came to the apothecary when they were sick. James came when his joints ached from the mill. Henry came when he had a cough. William came when his eyes were tired from reading. Charles came when he had a scrape or a bruise, which was often, because Charles was a boy who fell down and got up and fell down again. Archibald came because the apothecary was where the honey was, and Archibald, at two, had a relationship with honey that bordered on the devotional.

Elizabeth and Anne came for different reasons. Elizabeth came for the ledger — she kept the apothecary's accounts alongside the workshop's, and she was meticulous about both. Anne came for the stories. The apothecary was full of stories, because every jar had a history and every tincture had a name and every name had a person behind it. Anne would sit on the floor and listen while Aurelius told her about the woman who had brought him the willow bark, or the man who had taught him to distill rosemary, or the old woman who had given him the recipe for the salve that cured the miller's wife's hands.

Aurelius missed the apothecary. He missed it the way he missed the workshop, but differently — the workshop was his father's, and missing it was missing a man. The apothecary was his mother's, and missing it was missing a woman, and missing a woman who made tinctures and salves and quiet competence was a different kind of loss. It was the loss of softness. The castle site was all stone and wind and the king's demands. The apothecary was herbs and honey and the sound of Furball purring on a shelf.

He wrote to his wife about it. "I miss the apothecary," he said. "I miss the smell of the jars. I miss the way the light comes through the window in the afternoon and hits the ceramic and makes it glow. I miss Furball on the shelf. I miss the children coming in with their scrapes and their coughs and their questions. I miss the quiet."

His wife wrote back: "The apothecary is here. The jars are here. The light is here. Furball is here. The children are here. Everything is here, waiting. Build your castle. Come home."

### Chapter 12: The Spring

The second spring came, and the castle grew. The keep was rising — twelve feet of wall, round, solid, the stone fitted so tightly that you could not slip a knife between the courses. Aurelius had chosen the stone himself, from a quarry two miles down the valley. It was a grey limestone, hard and fine-grained, with a faint blue tint that came from the iron in the rock. He liked it. It was honest stone. It did not pretend to be something it was not.

The workers were better now. They had learned. They could lay a course without being told, could check a stone for cracks without being shown, could mix mortar to the right consistency by feel. Aurelius still supervised, but he supervised less. He walked the walls and looked and listened and occasionally tapped a stone with his hammer, and the sound told him whether it was sound. A solid stone rang. A cracked stone thudded. The difference was the difference between a castle that would stand and a castle that would fall, and Aurelius could hear it in his bones.

The king came less often now. He had other concerns — the enemy was moving, the border was restless, the treasury was stretched. He sent messages instead of visiting, and the messages said the same thing: "How is the castle? When will it be done? Are the walls thick enough? Can it withstand a siege?" Aurelius answered the messages, and his answers said the same thing: "The castle is rising. The walls are thick. It will be done when it is done. It will withstand what it is built to withstand."

The king did not like these answers. Aurelius did not care. The king's liking was not his concern. The castle was his concern, and the castle was good.

### Chapter 13: The Enemy

The enemy came in the autumn of the second year. Not to the castle — the castle was not finished — but to the border, to the north, to the road that the king had said they would come down. They came in force, with siege engines and ladders and men who knew how to use them. The king's army met them at the river, and there was a battle, and the battle was long and bloody and inconclusive, and when it was over, both sides withdrew, and the border held, and the castle on the hill stood unfinished and untested.

Aurelius watched the battle from the hill. He could see the smoke and the movement and the flash of steel, and he could hear the sound — a low, continuous roar, like the sea, like the wind, like something that was not human but was made of humans. He stood on the half-finished wall and watched, and he felt two things: relief that the enemy had not come to the castle, and shame that he was relieved.

The king came to the hill the next day. He looked tired. He looked older. The battle had taken something from him — not a wound, not a limb, but something internal, something that showed in the way he stood and the way he spoke.

"The castle," he said. "Is it ready?"

"No," Aurelius said. "It is not ready. The walls are half-finished. The keep is not complete. The gatehouse is not built. If the enemy comes here, the castle will not hold."

"Then make it hold."

### Chapter 21: The Return to the Apothecary

Aurelius returned to the apothecary the way a man returns to water after a long thirst. Not running, not rushing, but with the steady, deliberate pace of someone who knows that what he needs will be there when he arrives. The apothecary was there. The jars were there. The light was there. The mortar trough was there, empty, and the emptiness of it was a fact he accepted the way he accepted the weight of a stone — not with joy, not with grief, but with the understanding that weight is weight, and it must be carried.

He opened the door. The smell hit him first — not the smell of herbs and honey, which was what he expected, but the smell of dust and neglect. The apothecary had been unused for three years. The jars were still on the shelves, but the labels had faded. The tinctures had separated. The salves had dried. The lavender had lost its sweetness and become something flat and grey. The room was not ruined, but it was tired, the way a room gets tired when no one has lived in it.

Aurelius stood in the doorway and looked at it. He did not move for a long time. He let the disappointment come, and he let it go, and then he rolled up his sleeves and began.

The first thing he did was open the window. The air came in — cool, clean, carrying the smell of the garden and the sound of the chickens and the distant noise of the village. The second thing he did was take every jar off the shelf. He lined them up on the floor, in rows, and he began to sort. Some were good. Some were not. The tinctures that had separated could be re-mixed, shaken, strained. The salves that had dried could be re-melted, re-poured, re-set. The herbs that had lost their potency could be replaced — he would go to the fields, to the forest, to the market. He would gather what he needed. He would start again.

This was what Aurelius did. He started again. He had started again after the storm, after the fire, after the knock-out. He had started again after Furball died, after the siege, after the three years on the hill. He was a man who started again, and the starting was not a failure but a fact, and the fact was that things fall apart and things can be rebuilt, and the rebuilding is the work, and the work is the life.

Elizabeth came to the door. She stood there for a moment, watching him sort jars, and then she came in and began to help. She did not ask what to do. She saw what needed doing and she did it.
