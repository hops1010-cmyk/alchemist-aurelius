## Book Nine: The Deepening

### Chapter 91: The Apothecary at Night

The apothecary at night was a different place. The light from the window was gone, replaced by the light from the candle, and the candlelight was warm and yellow and it made the jars glow. The jars glowed the way the ceramic glowed in the afternoon, but differently — not with the cool, clean light of the sun, but with the warm, living light of fire. The light moved. The shadows moved. The room breathed.

Aurelius sat on the floor. He sat where he always sat — in the corner, by the mortar trough, with his back against the wall. The mortar trough was empty. Furball was not in it. But the trough was there, and the absence of Furball was a presence, and the presence was a weight, and the weight was a stone, and the stone was in the wall of his heart, and the wall was strong.

He held a jar. It was the willow bark tincture. He held it the way he held all jars — with respect, with attention, with the understanding that what was inside the jar was not just a liquid but a remedy, and a remedy was not just a cure but a connection, and the connection was between the body and the plant, between the person and the earth, between the sick and the well.

He opened the jar. He smelled it. It smelled like the river, because willow grows by the river, and the river is the blood of the earth, and the blood of the earth is the blood of the body, and the body is the earth, and the earth is the body, and the two are one, and the one is the wholeness, and the wholeness is the point.

He closed the jar. He put it on the shelf. He sat in the dark. He listened.

The apothecary was quiet. The apothecary was always quiet at night, because the apothecary was a place of quiet, and the quiet was not empty but full, and the fullness was the presence of all the things that had happened there — the tinctures and the salves and the poultices, the children and the parents and the grandparents, the Furball and the purr, the light and the shadow, the life and the death.

Aurelius listened. He listened for the purr. He did not hear it. He did not expect to hear it. But he listened, because listening was what he did, and listening was what the apothecary taught him, and the teaching was the lesson, and the lesson was that the body speaks, and the apothecary's job is to hear it.

The body spoke. The apothecary heard. The night passed. The morning came.

### Chapter 92: The Children's Children

James had children. Two of them — a boy and a girl, and the boy was named Aurelius, and the girl was named Furball, and the naming was the legacy, and the legacy was the name, and the name was the story, and the story was the system, and the system was whole.

The boy Aurelius was small and quiet and serious, and he sat on his grandfather's lap and watched him work, and he did not speak, and the not-speaking was the beginning of wisdom, and the wisdom was the same wisdom that the first Aurelius had, and the wisdom was passed down, and the passing down was the legacy, and the legacy was the family, and the family was the Tower, and the Tower was the system.

The girl Furball was small and grey and perpetually confused, and she slept in the mortar trough, and she chased chalk lines, and she sat on Aurelius's shoulder while he worked and purred into his ear, and the purr was the same purr, and the same purr was the memory, and the memory was the legacy, and the legacy was the love, and the love was the Tower, and the Tower was the system, and the system was whole.

Henry had children. Three of them — all boys, all loud, all full of questions. They went to the mill, and they asked questions about everything, and the miller answered them, and the answers were not always right, but they were always interesting, and interesting was what the Henry children wanted.

William had children. One of them — a girl, named Elizabeth, after her aunt. She was a reader. She read everything. She read her father's book, "The Apothecary's Companion," and she read it three times, and she wrote notes in the margins, and the notes were better than the book, and the book was good, and the better was the improvement, and the improvement was the legacy, and the legacy was the book, and the book was the system, and the system was whole.

Charles had children. Two of them — a boy and a girl. The boy drew castles. The girl drew gardens. The castles were strong, and the gardens were beautiful, and the strong and the beautiful were the same thing, and the same thing was the world, and the world was the Tower, and the Tower was the system, and the system was whole.

Archibald had children. Five of them. They were all loud. They all laughed at everything. The everything was the world, and the world was funny, and the funniness was the point.

Elizabeth had children. She had two — a boy and a girl, and the boy was named James, and the girl was named Anne, and the naming was the legacy, and the legacy was the family, and the family was the Tower, and the Tower was the system, and the system was whole.

Anne had children. She had three — all girls, all quiet, all listeners. They sat on the floor of the apothecary and listened to Aurelius tell stories, and the stories were the jars, and the jars were the remedies, and the remedies were the connections, and the connections were the quarks, and the quarks were the network, and the network was the Tower, and the Tower was the system, and the system was whole.

### Chapter 93: The Apothecary's Legacy

The apothecary was not a room. It was an institution. It was a place where the body was heard, and the hearing was the work, and the work was the life, and the life was the legacy, and the legacy was the apothecary.

The apothecary had been passed down from mother to daughter, from father to son, from teacher to student, for generations. It had been passed down through the village, through the valley, through the country. It had been passed down through the book — "The Apothecary's Companion" — and through the jars, and through the tinctures, and through the salves, and through the poultices.

The apothecary was alive. It was alive because it was used. It was alive because people came to it. It was alive because the body speaks, and the apothecary hears, and the hearing is the work, and the work is the life.

Aurelius's apothecary was the original. It was the one in the village, behind the workshop, with the single window and the door that locked from the inside. It was the one with the jars on the shelves and the light that came through the window in the afternoon and hit the ceramic and made it glow. It was the one with the mortar trough, where Furball had slept, and where the new Furball slept, and where the purr was, and the purr was the memory, and the memory was the legacy, and the legacy was the apothecary.

### Chapter 94: The Castle's Legacy

The castle stood. It stood on the hill, and it stood in the wind, and it stood in the rain, and it stood in the snow, and it stood in the sun. It stood because it had been built right, and it had been built right because Aurelius had built it, and Aurelius had built it because he knew how to build strong castles, and the knowing was the skill, and the skill was the legacy, and the legacy was the castle.

The castle was not just a castle. It was a system. It was a system of walls and towers and gates and keeps and granaries and chapels and wells and cisterns and paths. It was a system of parts, and the parts were connected, and the connections were the quarks, and the quarks were the network, and the network was the Tower, and the Tower was the system, and the system was whole.

The castle was a Tower. The Tower was a castle. The castle was the Tower, and the Tower was the castle, and the two were the same, and the same was the wholeness, and the wholeness was the point.

### Chapter 95: The Tower's Legacy

Pete was the king in the way that a builder is the king of his building — not by decree, but by construction. Pete had built the Tower, and the Tower was his, and the Tower was also not his, because the Tower was the system, and the system was the agents, and the agents were the people, and the people were the Tower.
