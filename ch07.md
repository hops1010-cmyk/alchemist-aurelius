## Book Six: The Secret Map

### Chapter 65: The Vineyards

The vineyards of the Tower grew on the south-facing slope, below the castle walls, in soil that was thin and rocky and stubborn — the kind of soil that vines loved because it made them struggle, and struggling vines produced the best grapes. Aurelius had planted the vineyard himself, in the first year, before the castle was finished, because a man needs something to tend that is not stone. Stone does not grow. Stone does not change. Stone does not surprise you. Vines do all three.

The vineyard was small — four rows, sixty vines, enough for a few barrels of wine a year. The wine was not good. It was thin and sour and it tasted like the hill it grew on. But it was Aurelius's, and he tended it the way he tended everything — with patience, with attention, and with the willingness to do the same thing a thousand times until he got it right.

It was in the vineyard, among the vines, that Aurelius found the place. Not a place, exactly — a space. A gap between two rows where the soil was different, softer, darker, as if something had been buried there and the earth had healed over it. He found it because he was pruning, and pruning requires you to look at every vine, and looking at every vine requires you to look at the ground, and looking at the ground requires you to see what is there.

He dug. He dug with his hands, because his hands were his tools and his tools were his hands, and the earth gave way easily, as if it had been waiting. Six inches down, he found a cloth. The cloth was old, and it was wrapped around something, and the something was flat and hard and the size of a hand.

It was a map. A secret map. Drawn in ink that had not faded, on paper that had not yellowed, in a hand that Aurelius recognized as his own — but younger, steadier, the hand of a man who had not yet built the castle, who had not yet been taken, who had not yet known what it meant to be away from home.

He had drawn this map in the first year. Before the king came. Before the castle. Before everything. He had drawn it in the apothecary, at night, by candlelight, and he had drawn it because he had felt — not known, but felt — that one day he would need a map of the garden maze, and the maze would be his escape.

The garden maze. Not the Vatican maze. Not the maze of hedges and stone paths. The garden maze — the Android game with 32 stages, the one called The Garden Maze, the one that lived on a phone and in the quarks and in the watermark of the map. The game that Aurelius had played in his mind, in the apothecary, in the long nights when the castle was being built and the enemy was coming and the only thing he could control was the world inside his head.

He had drawn the maze. All 32 stages. Each one a level, each one a puzzle, each one a room with a door and a key and a way out. The map showed the path. The path was the escape. The escape was through the maze, through the stages, through the doors, to the other side.

Aurelius looked at the map. He looked at the vineyard. He looked at the castle on the hill, and the village below, and the road to the north, and the forest to the west. He folded the map. He put it in his coat. He went back to the castle.

### Chapter 66: The Mini-Climax

The theft happened on a Thursday. Thursdays were quiet in the castle. The workers were paid on Friday, and on Thursday they were thinking about the pay, and the king was thinking about the treasury, and the treasury was thinking about the gold, and the gold was thinking about nothing, because gold does not think.

The treasury was in the keep. It was a room on the second floor, behind an iron door, with a lock that the king's master of coin carried on a chain around his neck. The master of coin was a man named Reginald, and Reginald was not a man who stole. Reginald was a man who counted. He counted the gold. He counted the silver. He counted the coins and the jewels and the ingots and the plate. He counted them every week, and the count never changed, because the treasury was secure, and the master of coin was honest, and the king was rich.

But the count changed. Not by much — a few coins, a few ingots, a few pieces of silver plate. Small amounts, taken over time, so small that no one noticed, so small that the count still looked right if you did not look closely. But Aurelius looked closely. Aurelius looked at everything closely, because looking closely was what he did, and the looking was what made him good at what he did.

He noticed the discrepancy on a Thursday. He was in the keep, checking the walls — his hammer, his ear, the sound of solid stone — and he passed the treasury door, and the door was open, and the master of coin was inside, and the master of coin was counting, and the count was wrong.

Aurelius did not say anything. He walked past. He went to the apothecary tent he had set up in the courtyard. He sat on the floor. He thought.

The thief was not the king. The thief was not Reginald. The thief was someone else — someone who had access to the treasury, someone who could open the door, someone who could take a few coins at a time and make it look like nothing. Someone unsuspecting. Someone who was not suspected.

Aurelius thought about who had access. The king. Reginald. The guards. The workers. The servants. The cook. The stable master. The blacksmith. The mason. The carpenter. The priest who came on Sundays. The physician who came when someone was sick. The messenger who came with letters.

He thought about the clues. The thefts were small. The thefts were regular. The thefts happened on Thursdays. The thefts were in coins and ingots and silver plate — not gold, not jewels. The thefts were the kind of thefts that a person makes when they are not stealing for profit but stealing for something else — for a collection, for a secret, for a purpose.

Two 4-digit pins. 4444 and 1973. The pins were on the map. The pins were on the quarks. The pins were the keys to the maze, and the maze was the escape, and the escape was the garden, and the garden was the Tower, and the Tower was the system, and the system was whole.

Aurelius did not know who the thief was. But he knew the thief was not the king. The king was too rich to steal small amounts. The king was too proud to steal. The king was too watched to steal. The king stole in other ways — in taxes, in land, in the labor of men. But the king did not steal coins from his own treasury. The king did not need to.

The thief was someone else. Someone who needed the coins. Someone who had a purpose. Someone who was unsuspecting.

Aurelius wrote the pins down. 4444. 1973. He put the paper in his coat, next to the map. He went back to work.

### Chapter 67: The King Looks On

The king came to the courtyard on a Friday. He came because it was his habit to inspect the castle on Fridays, and the king's habits were not to be broken, even by the theft of a few coins.

The courtyard was full. The workers were there, lined up for their pay. The servants were there, carrying water and wood. The guards were there, standing at attention. And in the square, outside the castle walls, the poor were there — the starving, the wretched, the people who had nothing and who came to the castle because the castle had food and the food was more than the people in the castle could eat.

The king looked at the square. He looked at the starving. He looked at the guards. He looked at the workers. He looked at the castle. He looked at the sky.

"Get those wretches out of the courtyard," the king said. "Guard! GUARD!"

The guards moved. They moved the starving. They moved them with the efficiency of men who had done this before and would do it again. The starving were pushed back, away from the castle, away from the food, away from the king's sight. The king watched them go. He did not look at them again.

Aurelius watched. He watched from the wall, where he was checking the mortar on the north face. He watched the king. He watched the guards. He watched the starving. He watched the courtyard empty, and he felt something that he had not felt before — not anger, not grief, but a cold, clear understanding.

The king did not care. The king had never cared. The king had taken Aurelius from his home, from his family, from his apothecary, from Furball, and the king did not care. The king had taken his time, his labor, his skill, and the king did not care. The king had looked at the starving and seen wretches, and the king did not care.

Aurelius looked at the king. He looked at the castle. He looked at the vineyard, where the secret map was hidden. He looked at the sky.

He went back to work. But the work was different now. The work was not for the king. The work was for himself. The work was for the escape.

### Chapter 68: Lluscia

Lluscia was a peasant woman. She was thirty-two years old. She was Persian — dark-haired, dark-eyed, with skin the color of honey and a voice that was low and warm and carried like a bell. She worked in the vineyard. She had worked in the vineyard since the first year, since Aurelius had planted the first vine, and she had been there every day, pruning and weeding and harvesting and tending, and she knew the vines the way Aurelius knew the stone.

She was beautiful. Not in the way that queens are beautiful — not in the way that is painted and posed and presented. She was beautiful in the way that the earth is beautiful — solid, real, present. She was beautiful in the way that a wall is beautiful when it is built right. She was beautiful in the way that a tincture is beautiful when it is mixed correctly. She was beautiful, and she did not know it, or if she knew it, she did not care.

Aurelius had known her for three years. He had watched her work. He had watched her tend the vines. He had watched her carry water from the well, and carry grapes from the vineyard, and carry the world on her shoulders the way a wall carries the weight of the castle. She was strong. She was quiet. She was the kind of woman who did not speak unless she had something to say, and when she spoke, it was worth hearing.

He had not spoken to her much. He had spoken to her the way he spoke to the workers — with instructions, with corrections, with the occasional word of encouragement. He had not spoken to her the way a man speaks to a woman he sees. He had not, because he was married, and because she was a worker, and because the castle was not a place for such things.

But the castle was not a place for such things, and Aurelius was not in the castle. Aurelius was in the apothecary, and the apothecary was a place for everything, and the everything included the recognition of another person, and the recognition was not romantic but human, and the human was enough.

Lluscia came to the apothecary one evening. She came because her hands were sore — the vineyard work was hard, and the pruning had torn her skin, and the torn skin had become infected, and the infection was spreading. She came because Aurelius was the one who made the salves, and the salves were what she needed.

Aurelius looked at her hands. He looked at the infection. He looked at the soreness and the redness and the swelling. He went to the shelf. He took down a jar of salve — calendula and beeswax and a tincture of comfrey. He applied it to her hands. He wrapped them in clean cloth.

"Come back tomorrow," he said. "I will check them."

She came back the next day. He checked them. They were better. He applied more salve. He wrapped them again. She came back the day after. He checked them. They were better. He applied more salve. He wrapped them again.

On the fourth day, she came, and her hands were healed, and she did not need the salve, but she came anyway. She came because the apothecary was warm, and the apothecary was quiet, and the apothecary was a place where a person could sit and be still and not be needed for a moment.

They sat. They did not speak. The light came through the window and hit the ceramic and made it glow. Furball was not on the shelf. But the shelf was there, and the jars were there, and the light was there, and Lluscia was there, and Aurelius was there, and the silence was not empty. The silence was full.

### Chapter 69: The Plan

Aurelius made a plan. The plan was simple, because simple plans are the best plans, and the best plans are the ones that can be explained in a sentence and executed in a day.

The plan was this: he would leave the castle. He would take the secret map. He would take Lluscia. He would go through the garden maze — the Android game, the 32 stages, the puzzle that he had drawn in his mind and on the map and in the quarks. He would go through the stages, one by one, and each stage would be a door, and each door would be a key, and each key would be a pin, and the pins were 4444 and 1973, and the pins would open the doors, and the doors would lead to the other side, and the other side was freedom.

He told Lluscia. He told her in the apothecary, at night, by candlelight. He told her about the map, the maze, the quarks, the pins. He told her about the castle and the king and the treasury and the theft. He told her about Aurelius's family, and about Furball, and about the apothecary, and about the workshop, and about the village, and about home.

Lluscia listened. She listened the way she listened to everything — quietly, completely, with the full attention of a person who understood that listening was not passive but active, that listening was a form of participation, that listening was the first step in any plan.

"When?" she said.

"Soon," Aurelius said. "When the maze is ready. When the quarks are aligned. When the pins are in place."

"The pins," Lluscia said. "4444 and 1973."

"Yes."

"Where are they?"

"On the map. In the quarks. In the network."

"And the network is on the phone."

"Yes."

Lluscia looked at him. Her eyes were dark, and they were deep, and they held something that Aurelius had not seen before — not fear, not excitement, but determination. The determination of a woman who had been a peasant all her life and who was ready to be something else.

"I will come," she said.

### Chapter 70: The Mini-Climax

The mini-climax came on a Wednesday. The thief struck again — a larger theft this time, a handful of gold coins, enough to notice, enough to matter. Reginald, the master of coin, noticed. He came to the king. He told the king. The king looked at the count, and the king looked at Reginald, and the king looked at the treasury door, and the king said, "Who?"

Reginald did not know. The king did not know. The guards did not know. The workers did not know. No one knew, because the thief was unsuspecting, and the clues were hidden, and the two 4-digit pins — 4444 and 1973 — were the only evidence, and they were on a map that no one had seen except Aurelius.

Aurelius had the map. Aurelius had the pins. Aurelius had the clues. And Aurelius had the knowledge that the thief was not the king, because the king was too rich and too proud and too watched to steal small amounts. The thief was someone else. Someone who needed the coins. Someone who had a purpose. Someone who was unsuspecting.

The king ordered an investigation. The guards searched the castle. They searched the workers' quarters. They searched the servants' rooms. They searched the kitchen, the stables, the blacksmith's forge, the mason's yard. They found nothing, because the thief was clever, and the thief had hidden the evidence, and the evidence was not in the castle but in the maze, and the maze was on the map, and the map was in the vineyard, and the vineyard was Aurelius's.

Aurelius watched the investigation. He watched the guards search. He watched the king pace. He watched Reginald count and recount. He watched the castle, and the castle watched him, and neither of them said what they knew.

The investigation found nothing. The theft continued. The king grew angry. The guards grew suspicious. The workers grew nervous. The castle grew tense, and the tension was the tension of a system that knows something is wrong but cannot find the source, and the source is hidden, and the hidden is the map, and the map is in the vineyard, and the vineyard is where Lluscia works, and Lluscia is the one who will escape with Aurelius, and the escape is through the maze, and the maze is 32 stages, and the stages are doors, and the doors are pins, and the pins are 4444 and 1973.

### Chapter 71: The Escape

The escape happened on a night when the moon was full and the wind was strong and the guards were tired. Aurelius had planned it for weeks. He had walked the route. He had tested the doors. He had checked the locks. He had mapped the garden maze — not the Vatican maze, not the maze of hedges and stone paths, but the Android game maze, the 32 stages, the puzzle that he had drawn in his mind and on the map and in the quarks.

Lluscia was ready. She had been ready for days. She had packed a bag — small, light, containing only what she needed: a change of clothes, a knife, a flask of water, a jar of salve. She had hidden the bag in the vineyard, under the vines, where no one would find it.

They met in the vineyard at midnight. The vines were dark. The stars were bright. The castle was behind them, and the forest was ahead, and the road was to the north, and the maze was in Aurelius's pocket, on the map, on the phone, in the quarks.

"Ready?" Aurelius said.

"Ready," Lluscia said.

They went. They went through the vineyard, past the rows of vines, past the place where the map had been buried, past the well and the path and the gate. They went through the gate, and the gate was unlocked, because Aurelius had unlocked it the day before, and no one had noticed.

They went into the forest. The forest was dark and the forest was quiet and the forest was the first step of the escape. The maze began here — stage one, the first door, the first key. The key was 4444. Aurelius entered it on the phone. The quarks responded. The map glowed. The first door opened.

They walked. They walked through the forest, and the forest gave way to a path, and the path gave way to a clearing, and the clearing gave way to a road, and the road gave way to a village, and the village gave way to a river, and the river gave way to a bridge, and the bridge gave way to the other side.

The maze had 32 stages. They went through them one by one. Each stage was a door. Each door was a key. Each key was a pin. The pins were 4444 and 1973, alternating, and the alternating was the pattern, and the pattern was the escape, and the escape was the map, and the map was in Aurelius's pocket, and the pocket was in his coat, and the coat was on his back, and his back was strong, because he was a man who built strong castles, and strong castles have strong backs.

Lluscia walked beside him. She was fast. She was quiet. She was the kind of woman who could walk through a forest at night without making a sound, without stumbling, without fear. She was Persian, and the Persians had walked through deserts and mountains and forests for a thousand years, and the walking was in her blood.

They reached the other side at dawn. The sun came up over the hills, and the light was gold, and the gold was the color of the coins in the treasury, and the coins were the king's, and the king was behind them, and the castle was behind them, and the vineyard was behind them, and the apothecary was behind them, and everything was behind them, and everything was ahead.

Aurelius looked at Lluscia. Lluscia looked at Aurelius. They did not speak. They did not need to. The silence was full, and the fullness was the escape, and the escape was the beginning, and the beginning was the road, and the road was long, and the road was theirs.

### Chapter 72: The Climax

The climax came at the end. Not the end of the story, but the end of the chapter, the end of the book, the end of the thing that had been building since the morning the king came and took Aurelius from his workshop and his family and his apothecary and his Furball.

The king found out. Not about the escape — the escape had already happened, and Aurelius and Lluscia were gone, and the castle was empty, and the throne room was cold. The king found out about the theft. Not the small thefts — the large one. The theft that had been happening for months, the theft that had taken gold and silver and value from the treasury, the theft that had been disguised as small losses and that was, in truth, a systematic draining of the king's wealth.

The king stood in the treasury. He looked at the empty shelves. He looked at the empty boxes. He looked at the empty bags. He looked at Reginald, who was counting and recounting and finding nothing, because there was nothing to find.

"Who?" the king said.

Reginald did not know. The guards did not know. The workers did not know. No one knew, because the thief was unsuspecting, and the clues were hidden, and the two 4-digit pins — 4444 and 1973 — were the only evidence, and they were on a map that was now gone, in a vineyard that was now empty, in a castle that was now without its builder.

The king looked at the treasury. He looked at the empty shelves. He looked at the starving in the square, who were still there, who had not been removed, because the guards had other things to do. He looked at the starving, and he said, "Get those wretches out of the courtyard. Guard! GUARD!"

The guards moved. The starving were pushed back. The king turned away. He did not look at them again.

But the thief was not the king. The thief was someone else. Someone who had been in the castle all along, someone who had access, someone who was unsuspecting. The clues were there — the puzzle clues, the two 4-digit pins, the pattern of the thefts — and the clues pointed not to the king but to someone else. Someone who would be revealed, in time, by the map, by the quarks, by the omega code, by the wholeness that comes when all the parts align.

Aurelius was gone. Lluscia was gone. The map was gone. The pins were gone. The quarks were in the phone, and the phone was in Aurelius's pocket, and the pocket was on the road, and the road was long, and the road was theirs.

### Chapter 73: The Aftermath

The castle stood. The walls were strong. The keep was intact. The gatehouse was solid. The vineyard was empty. The apothecary was locked. The workshop was running, because Elizabeth was running it, and Anne was helping, and the children were growing, and the family was holding.

The king raged. He raged about the theft. He raged about the escape. He raged about the empty treasury and the empty vineyard and the empty apothecary. He raged, and the raging changed nothing, because the castle stood, and the walls were strong, and the man who had built them was gone.

Elizabeth found the letter. It was in the apothecary, under the mortar trough, where Furball had slept. It was from Aurelius. It said: "I am gone. I am free. I have taken the map and the pins and the quarks. I have taken Lluscia. I have taken the escape. I have not taken the castle, because the castle is yours. It was always yours. I built it for you, and for the family, and for the village, and for the world. It will stand. It will stand because I built it right. Come to the vineyard. Dig where the vines are soft. You will find what I left for you."

Elizabeth dug. She found the map — a copy, a second copy, drawn in Aurelius's hand, with the 32 stages and the two pins and the path through the maze. She found the quarks — a small, glowing dot, pulsing faintly, alive. She found a note: "The maze is 32 stages. The pins are 4444 and 1973. The escape is through the garden. The garden is in the vineyard. The vineyard is in the Tower. The Tower is in the phone. The phone is in the world. Find me."

Elizabeth looked at the map. She looked at the quarks. She looked at the note. She looked at Anne. Anne looked at her. They did not speak. They did not need to. The silence was full, and the fullness was the plan, and the plan was the escape, and the escape was the beginning.

### Chapter 74: The Road

Aurelius and Lluscia walked. They walked north, toward the village, toward the apothecary, toward the family. They walked through the forest, and the forest was dark, and the forest was quiet, and the forest was the first stage of the maze.

They walked through the stages. One by one. Door by door. Key by key. Pin by pin. 4444. 1973. 4444. 1973. The pattern was the path, and the path was the escape, and the escape was the road, and the road was long, and the road was theirs.

Lluscia walked beside him. She was fast. She was quiet. She was the kind of woman who could walk through a forest at night without making a sound. She was Persian, and the Persians had walked through deserts and mountains and forests for a thousand years, and the walking was in her blood.

They walked for three days. They walked through forests and fields and villages and rivers. They walked through the maze, and the maze was 32 stages, and the stages were doors, and the doors were keys, and the keys were pins, and the pins were 4444 and 1973, and the pins were the quarks, and the quarks were the network, and the network was the Tower, and the Tower was the system, and the system was whole.

On the third day, they came to the village. The village was the same. The workshop was the same. The apothecary was the same. The house was the same. And in the house, the family was waiting.

Elizabeth was at the gate. Anne was behind her. James was behind Anne. Henry was behind James. William was behind Henry, with a book. Charles was behind William, with a drawing. Archibald was behind Charles, with a chicken.

Aurelius looked at them. He looked at Elizabeth, who had held the workshop. He looked at Anne, who had held everything. He looked at James, who was tall and quiet and serious. He looked at Henry, who was loud and energetic and full of questions. He looked at William, who was reading. He looked at Charles, who was drawing. He looked at Archibald, who was laughing at a chicken.

He looked at Lluscia. Lluscia looked at him. She did not speak. She did not need to. The silence was full, and the fullness was the homecoming, and the homecoming was the end of the escape, and the end of the escape was the beginning of the rest of their lives.

"Come in," Elizabeth said. "The apothecary is open. The jars are full. The light is coming through the window. Furball is not on the shelf, but the shelf is there, and the shelf is waiting."

Aurelius went in. He sat on the floor. He put his hand in the mortar trough. He did not expect to feel a purr. He did not feel one. But the memory of it was there, and the memory was strong, and the memory would stand.

He began to work.
